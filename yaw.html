Drive System Considerations

A direct-drive system was selected for its ease of maintenance and low potential for failure points. The Damiao DL10010L Motor was selected as the drive motor due to its capability to handle the required drive torque, as well as its ease of integration with Calibur Robotics’ current hardware infrastructure, with their extensive use of Damiao motors. The interim report covers more details on the selection considerations.

Electronics Bay

An electronics bay was designed and equipped on the yaw subsystem as well, to better house all electronics and PCBs required for the robot’s operations, and act as a central node for wires to route to. The LCD Touch Screen, covered in later sections, is also mounted on the frame of the robot.

Potential Improvements

The current electronics bay is not without its flaws, and can be improved by, 1. Improving wire routing to be more professional. Current wires are carelessly routed out from the box through generic holes cutout from the walls of the electronics bay. a. Solution: Introduce dedicated wire routing outlets that are able to protect the wires upon exit from the bay. These wires should remain protected and routed in a way that is neat and does not interrupt the dynamics of the robot. 2. Improvements to electronic bay cover. The covers should be allowed to open for access to the internal electronics a. Include hinges into design, and locking mechanisms to hold covers open or closed.

Final Design of Yaw Subsystem

Final Direct V3

The interactive above shows the final design of the yaw subsystem. The subsystem on it’s own is able to support full rotational motion, excluding the sector the LCD screen is in. This range of motion will be further reduced upon integration, but it fulfils our yaw range requirements of 2.2(anti-clockwise) to -7.8(clockwise) degrees.
The subsystem is also able to fully support the expected load from the other subsystems that will be mounted on the yaw subsystem.

Integration with other subsystems

The Yaw subsystem is designed to directly integrate with the pitch subsystem, and the upper assembly, which consists of the launcher and feeder subsystems, will be mounted on the pitch subsystem. The figure below shows the pitch subsystem integrated with the yaw subsystem.

Upon integration with the other subsystems, the range of yaw motion is restricted to a -10 to 10-degree sector, with the 0-degree reference being the launcher parallel to the yaw frame, and pointing towards the direction of launch for the projectile.

Validation and testing

Functionality testing

The basic functionality testing includes the movement of the yaw. The yaw subsystem was able to move its position freely within the -10 to 10 degree sector, without any interference with wires, electronics, or other components of the Dart robot. This is also accomplished without any failure of structural components.
With that, we show that the dart robot is capable of achieving the target yaw angles of 2.2 and -7.8 degrees as required by the RMUC competition rules.

It is worth noting that while there was no immediate failure, after many repetitions of use, and movement from location to location, fatigue was observed at the standoffs situated near the frame. Specifically, the 3D printed component that acted as a spacer had started to show signs of fatigue, and can be seen to be crushed under the weight of the launcher.
While this does not cause the functionality of the yaw or overall functionality of any components to cease, it does affect the accuracy and reliability of the robot in aiming of the projectile. Furthermore, it is likely that the fatigue of this component contributes to the swaying phenomenon when an external force is used to shake the robot.
A solution to this will be to make use of a stronger material that is less likely to fatigue under the expected load, or reconsider the settings and print orientation used when 3D printing the spacer to achieve a stronger 3D printed component.

Validation: Repeatability

A controlled test to validate the repeatability of the yaw subsystem to reach target angles was conducted. Repeatability is a high priority for the robot, as the robot is responsible for aiming the projectile towards the target structure, and moving to a target yaw angle will be needed each time the robot is moved to and from the starting position in the competition.
In our test, we directly controlled the yaw motor and drew angular data from the motor through the development board via live expressions in the STM32 Cube IDE. We first chose a zero position, which was designated to be the center position allowing the launcher to be parallel to the yaw frames. Next, we move the yaw position away from the zero position, before inputting the chosen zero position as the target angle. The yaw is allowed to adjust freely to the target position, and the final yaw angle is observed from live expressions and recorded.
