Launcher Electronics

Design Requirements

Reliable launcher operation depends on four core functional elements: measuring force, detecting latch engagement, actuating the release mechanism, and driving the linear rail with a motor.

System requirements

The first bring-up goal was to exercise the launcher alone: prove the carriage could draw elastic preload under motor control, that a latch could release on command, and that an operator could stop the rig quickly—without debugging feeder indexing or multi-subsystem timing. That deliberately shrank the failure domain while rail travel, band sets, and latch geometry were still changing weekly.

Interim reasoning (pre–full onboarding) favoured a solenoid for release because it offered the shortest path to a software-triggered “fire,” minimal PWM choreography, and straightforward bench wiring next to a relay for clean energisation of the coil. A remote controller was included for manual draw/release and emergency stop authority during mechanical experiments. Those choices were plausible from datasheets and intent; whether release stayed reliable at depth under real band load was left to bench testing—documented in the testing subsection below.

Test methodology

Test evidence — solenoid release bench

Following this issue, the solenoid lock was tested at different pullback distances to determine the release voltage required at each position. A benchtop power supply was used to vary the input voltage during testing.

In each trial, the lock was pulled to a set distance, and the voltage was increased until release occurred. The test was then repeated for multiple distances to study the relationship between pullback distance and required release voltage.

At a pullback distance of 0.25 m, even 24 V was not sufficient to release the band. Since 24 V was the maximum output of the benchtop power supply, it was judged to be unwise to operate the solenoid lock beyond its rated voltage in an attempt to force release.

The test results also showed that the voltage required for release increased exponentially with pullback distance. In addition, the pullback distance needed from the band exceeded the practical operating range of the solenoid lock. This showed that the solenoid-based release method became unreliable after a certain distance and was therefore not suitable for the launcher.

As a result, a different release solution had to be implemented in V2.

Interpretation: if increasing voltage mainly trades heat and inrush current for small gains in release reliability—while deep draws remain a moving target—then a position-controlled servo is a better match to the final requirement for repeatable, tunable release under varying preload.

V2 introduces the release-mechanism change and force-reading strategy update after V1 solenoid release limitations were observed.

Solenoid lock unlocking -> servo triggering the mechanical unlocking mechanism

After it was deemed that the solenoid lock unlocking mechanism does not work, a change in releasing mechanism was required. This came in the form of using a servo to pull the releasing mechanism. This mechanism worked in releasing the solenoid at the distance needed.

Load cell force reading -> launching motor torque reading

The interim (V0) architecture assumed explicit tension / firing-force sensing—commonly a load-cell path alongside the launcher. That path was removed in favour of launcher motor torque and CAN telemetry (see the evolution strip: “load-cell path retired in favour of launcher motor torque telemetry”). The same “measure force” requirement from the design block is still met, but without a separate strain-gauge channel on the rail.

V3 is the inclusion of the feeder: launcher and feeder are no longer brought up in isolation—timing, datums, and shared state must line up. Early solenoid bench characterisation remains under V1.

Extra signals

The integrated stack needs additional inputs: distance from the optical sensor, position of the feeder motor, and delays for servos so datum alignment, indexing, and fire sequencing stay coherent across both subsystems.

Software integration

More code was written to integrate feeder and launcher firmware that had been written independently: shared state, hand-offs, and coordination so the combined behaviour matches the full-robot electrical and mechanical intent.

V4 is additional code written to aid tuning—supporting faster iteration when calibrating parameters and checking behaviour on the bench. Bring-up bench evidence for the solenoid rail remains under V1.
