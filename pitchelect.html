Pitch Electronics

Controlling elevation with precision, the pitch electronics keep launcher motion sharp, stable, and reliable. The design emphasizes strict operational limits, high-resolution position tracking, and responsive operator interaction to coordinate accurate vertical target acquisition.

Design requirements

Angular envelope

Commanded pitch must stay between 35° and 45°—a 10° working span—so the launcher cannot strike structure or violate the mechanical hard stops.

Electrical supply

The motor must run from the available 24 V bus; the driver is selected for continuous and peak current at that voltage.

Resolution

Feedback must resolve small pitch changes so aim adjustments on the order of a fraction of a degree remain meaningful after gear reduction.

Datum and zeroing

After homing, the quadrature counter must be referenced to 35° so all setpoints lie in a consistent frame.

Counter integrity

Position integration must not overflow during the longest commanded move; timer/counter hardware must cover the worst-case count excursion.

Operator interface

Pitch commands must be accepted from the touchscreen or from stored presets with identical limiting behaviour.

Components and selection

Pitch hardware and datasheet details ⌄
  • Brushed DC motor: chosen for the torque constant and thermal margin at 24 V within the pitch linkage.
  • Brushed DC motor driver: matched to stall current, PWM frequency, and logic-level control from the STM32.
  • Hall-effect encoder and gearing: stages of 1:50 and 1:100 with 34 poles / 17 pole pairs and quadrature decoding yield 340 000 counts per revolution of the output shaft in the documented arithmetic.
  • STM32F4 encoder interface: quadrature channels are wired to the peripheral encoder inputs so hardware maintains the count rather than bit-banging edges under interrupt load.
  • TIM2 (32-bit): used as the timing/count resource associated with the encoder channel so wrap distance exceeds any single manoeuvre in the 10° window.
  • Optical endstop: provides a repeatable mechanical reference during homing before the software applies the 35° zero offset.
  • Pitch PCB: routes motor power, encoder signals, endstop, and logic grounds with separation to limit switching noise on the sensor lines.

Implementation

At initialisation the motor drives slowly toward the optical stop; when the beam breaks, firmware records the mechanical reference and applies the offset that maps the quadrature counter to 0 at 35° physical pitch. All further commands are clamped to 35°–45°. Setpoints from the operator UI and from non-volatile presets pass through the same limiter so behaviour is identical in competition and bench modes.

Each encoder increment represents 360/340 000 degrees. The full 10° span therefore corresponds to about 10 ÷ (360/340 000) ≈ 9 444 counts—far below the 2³²−1 capacity of TIM2, so counter overflow is not expected for this axis.

Testing

Homing repeatability

Multiple power cycles record the encoder count at endstop capture; spread in captured values indicates mechanical backlash or sensor placement tolerance.

Software limits at 35° and 45°

Commands beyond each bound are issued from the UI; firmware must clamp without windup and the measured encoder position must remain inside the allowed count band derived from 360/340 000 per tick.

Span vs predicted counts

A slow sweep from 35° to 45° is compared against the theoretical ≈9 444 count delta to validate gear documentation and wiring polarity.

Encoder peripheral vs interrupt counting

Stress tests with rapid dither at the linkage verify the hardware counter matches a reference trace without dropped quadrature edges—something difficult to guarantee with GPIO interrupt service alone at high edge rates.

UI and preset equivalence

The same numeric target is applied from live touchscreen entry and from recall of a stored preset; resulting steady-state encoder counts must match within one LSB after settling.
