Methodology used

The development process followed a comprehensive V-shaped system methodology. This approach enabled the structured design, implementation, and rigorous unit testing of each distinct hardware and software (yaw, pitch, launcher, feeder) subsystem in isolation. Once isolated tests were verified, these elements were incrementally integrated and validated on the opposite side of the "V", culminating in a robust, fully functional final system.

Design requirements

Yaw travel envelope

Approximately 7.8° to one side and 2.2° to the other from centre, matching the mechanical aim window without exceeding bearing or cable-wrap limits.

Operator safety

Software limits prevent the turret from entering the lateral space where teammates or judges can stand; commands that would violate the envelope are rejected or clamped.

Closed-loop position

Encoder feedback is required so yaw holding torque and drift are compensated during long match segments.

Human–machine interface

Operators need a single surface to adjust pitch, yaw, and fire-related parameters without recompiling firmware mid-event.

Integration with competition stack

The RoboMaster development Type A electronics must interface cleanly to the custom wire-management PCB so competition harnesses remain serviceable.

Yaw actuator — Damiao DM-J10010L-2EC

Integrated brushless servo actuator (motor + driver + encoders)

Selected for continuous torque headroom on the yaw linkage and built-in dual magnetic encoders so the axis does not rely on a separate encoder assembly. Control uses the onboard CAN interface at 1 Mbps; parameter tuning uses UART at 921600 baud per the manufacturer documentation.

Operator display — capacitive vs resistive touch

4.0″ class modules · SPI LCD · touch overlay technology trade-off

The pit crew UI needs a readable panel that works with gloved or dry fingers under time pressure. Two representative 4.0″ module styles were compared: a capacitive SPI TFT with I²C touch (ST7796S + FT6336U class, as documented on LCD Wiki — 4.0″ capacitive SPI ST7796) and a conventional resistive 4.0″ assembly that relies on pressure and often ships with a stylus.

Why capacitive was chosen

For field adjustments next to a running launcher, the team prioritized fast, low-effort taps without hunting for a stylus, plus a brighter, cleaner readout for numbers under venue lighting. The 4.0″ 320×480 capacitive SPI module family on LCD Wiki documents a compact 14-pin header (LCD SPI, backlight, SD_CS, and CTP I²C), onboard level shifting for 5 V / 3.3 V MCUs, and an SD slot for assets—matching the RoboMaster controller wiring budget better than a resistive panel that pushes the operator toward stylus-based workflows. Resistive remains a valid low-cost fallback where gloves are thick or where EMI noise on the analog sense lines is easier to manage than capacitive moisture edge cases.

Implementation

Firmware enforces asymmetric angular limits corresponding to 7.8° and 2.2° so the launcher centroid stays inside the safety sector. Encoder readings close the loop on the driver; when a touchscreen command would exceed either bound, the command scaler reduces magnitude while preserving sign, or drops the update and flags the operator.

The touchscreen application mirrors critical parameters also stored as presets so a field technician can recover from a corrupted page by reloading defaults from flash. Pitch and yaw pages share navigation chrome for consistency under stress.

Testing

Yaw limit enforcement

With the mechanism on a fixture, ramped position commands are sent past each software stop; logged encoder position must plateau at the configured 7.8° / 2.2° boundaries without chatter or limit cycling.

Encoder and holding torque

A constant side load is applied while yaw is commanded to hold; drift rate and recovery after release characterise whether 14-bit feedback is sufficient for the regulator gains chosen.

Touchscreen workflow

Operators step through pitch, yaw, and fire pages under time pressure; incorrect entries are deliberately injected to confirm validation messages and that illegal combinations cannot be committed to RAM.

Harness continuity

Ring-out from Dev A through the wire-management PCB to the yaw driver confirms continuity and correct pairing of power, ground, and differential or single-ended sense lines before full-motion commissioning.
